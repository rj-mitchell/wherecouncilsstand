AI briefings

Briefings are written by AI.

What the model is given

The AI is given only the council minutes and votes found for your question, and may use nothing else. It is told to treat anything that isn’t in those passages as unknown, even well-known facts. The passages are listed under the briefing, each linked to the council’s own minutes.

Evidence is drawn from across councils run by different parties. It is taken in turn from councils under each party’s control and from councils under no overall control, with at most two passages from any one council. The same rules apply to every party: see Fair to every party.

The site holds every council minute and named vote it reads, from May 2018, all searchable and linked: full council and cabinet meetings, and the boards and scrutiny committees of England’s strategic authorities. The list of councils shows which councils are covered so far, and the reasons for the rest. A question searches the last four years unless it asks about earlier ones. Councils’ minutes are read in every day, and recent minutes are read again in case they have changed.

How it is checked

Each point links to its source. Citations are checked against the source text, quotations are checked word for word, and sentences that no source supports are flagged. A flagged sentence stays in the briefing, marked, so the reader can see which parts are supported.

If the record holds nothing relevant, the site says “No records found”. Where the search finds no minutes at all, no briefing is written. Where it finds minutes that don’t answer the question, the AI is told to say so, and to say what they do cover.

The method and the code are published, every briefing is labelled as written by AI, and the site is free and doesn’t track you. The method is on the methodology page, the code is open source, and the privacy page says what is kept.
