Methodology

Where Councils Stand reads the minutes councils already publish, and writes briefings in which every point cites those minutes.

Where the record comes from

Most councils in the UK publish their meetings through one of three systems: Modern.gov and CMIS, both made by Civica, and CoINS, made by Northgate. Where Councils Stand reads all three. Each Modern.gov site has a public web service that gives every meeting’s agenda, the minutes of each item, and the councillors listed at the meeting with their party on the day. CMIS and CoINS sites have no such service, so their public pages are read as a browser would read them. Each council is sent one request at a time, at least a second apart (longer if its site asks), by a reader that names itself, and a copy is kept so that each meeting is fetched once. If a council’s site refuses these requests, the council is asked for permission.

It covers full council and cabinet meetings since May 2018, including annual, budget and extraordinary meetings. Councils run by committees, which have no cabinet, are covered through the committees that make their main policy and budget decisions, usually a policy and resources committee. Other committees, cabinet members’ individual decisions, and planning and licensing are not included.

Where a council publishes a meeting’s minutes only as a document, the document is read instead. Some councils’ sites refuse automated requests for documents, so some meetings have no minutes here. The list of councils shows which councils are covered, and why the rest aren’t yet.

The record is brought up to date every night. New meetings are added, and minutes from the last four months are read again, because minutes can change until they are approved at the next meeting.

Strategic authorities

England’s strategic authorities (combined authorities, combined county authorities and the Greater London Authority) take decisions on transport, skills, housing and investment across several councils. The site covers those whose meetings are published through Modern.gov, and four that publish them as documents on their own websites (Tees Valley, Hull and East Yorkshire, Cumbria, and Hampshire and the Solent), whose minutes are read from those documents: their board (called the combined authority, the board or the cabinet), including budget meetings, their overview and scrutiny committees, and the mayor’s decisions where the meetings system publishes them, from May 2018 or the authority’s creation. Each authority’s page gives its kind, its constituent councils and its mayors, and each council’s page says which authority it is part of. The list says which are covered, and why the rest aren’t yet.

A strategic authority isn’t run by a party. Where it has a mayor, the mayor’s party on the date is shown in its place, always labelled as the mayor’s (“Mayor: Labour”), and briefings group authorities by their mayor’s party, never together with councils under a party’s control. One without a mayor has no single party: its members are appointed by its councils, each with the same say. In a briefing, each authority counts as one body, like a council.

Board members are mostly council leaders, appointed by their councils. Each is shown with their role (the mayor, a constituent or non-constituent council’s member, a substitute or a co-opted member) and their party on the date, taken from their own council’s record where the site has it, and otherwise from the authority’s. At the Greater London Authority, the elected London Assembly members are named too, with the party the GLA records. Only councillors, Assembly members and the mayor are named: appointed members who aren’t councillors, such as a business board’s chair, are counted but not named.

Minutes

Minutes are a summary of a meeting written by the council’s officers. Briefings therefore say what the minutes record, and attribute that summary to the minutes. Quotations are only ever of words in the minutes.

Party on the date, and party in control

Each meeting’s list of councillors records each councillor’s party at the time, so a councillor who changed party is shown with the party they had at each meeting. Councils label parties in many ways (“LAB”, “Labour”, “Lab & Co-op”). These are mapped to one name per party, partly by rules and partly from Open Council Data’s lists of councillors. Named local groups of independents or residents count as local parties, each under its own name, separately from councillors listed as Independent.

A party is in overall control when it has more than half the councillors listed at a full council meeting. Otherwise the council is under no overall control. This is reported as the council recorded it, as a fact about the date. For strategic authorities, see above.

Named votes

When a council takes a recorded vote, the minutes list who voted for, against and abstained. Councils write these lists in at least nine different ways. Each name is matched to the councillors listed at the meeting, so each vote shows every councillor’s party on the date. Names that can’t be matched are shown as written.

The reading has been checked by hand against the minutes: 300 named votes, drawn at random in three samples of 100. The first sample found 9 votes with a councillor on the wrong side. After the reading was fixed, the second and third found 2 each, every time from a way of writing the list not seen before. Those are now read correctly or left out. A vote is also left out when the names read don’t match a count printed in the minutes, or when it puts one councillor on two sides.

Even so, expect about 1 vote in 100 to have a councillor on the wrong side. That is accepted while the site is in alpha, and it is being worked on: before the beta, a fixed set of checked votes will be built, so that every change to the reading is tested against it. If only one side of a vote could be read, the page says so. Each vote links to the council’s own minutes, so you can check it, and please report any mistake you find.

Writing a briefing

  1. The question is read into filters: dates, councils, the party in control and kinds of meeting. By default the last four years are searched; a question about earlier years goes back to May 2018. The filters used are shown above the briefing.
  2. The minutes are searched, both by keyword and by meaning, using a model that runs on the site’s own server.
  3. The passages found are grouped by the party in overall control of each council on the date (a strategic authority by its mayor’s party, in a group of its own), and drawn from each group in turn, at most two from any one council or authority. The same rules apply to every party.
  4. An AI model writes the briefing from those passages only. It is told to report what the minutes record, in neutral words, and each point cites the passage it comes from. A council’s control is not the view of everyone who speaks there: each passage is given to the model with the party of each councillor it names on the date, and of the council’s directly elected mayor where the council’s own attendance lists show one, and the model is told never to present a point as the party in control’s unless the person who made it belongs to that party. Under each group’s heading, the list of sources gives the people in each passage with their own party.
  5. The briefing is checked. Every citation is checked against its passage, and quotations against the minutes. The counts are shown at the top of the briefing, under “Checked against the council minutes”. A quotation found word for word is set in the minutes’ typeface on a tinted background, with “✓ word for word”; a quotation not found word for word in the minutes cited for it has a wavy underline and “Not found word for word”. Text that no source supports has a dotted underline, and the first such passage says “No source”; so does text whose citations all failed the check (they are removed). Such text is always shown, never hidden. A quotation found word for word in the minutes cited straight after it is shown as those minutes’ quotation, even where the citation was attached to the words after it. Loaded words in the model’s own voice are flagged.
  6. After each point, a label names the minutes it cites: the council, the meeting and the date. On a computer the passage itself is shown beside the briefing, with the quoted words highlighted; on a phone, pressing the label opens it.

If the minutes hold nothing relevant, the briefing says “No records found”. Briefings can still be wrong. Please check the sources, and tell us if something is wrong.

Topic briefings

The topics are ten standing questions on what councils keep returning to, each asking what councils have discussed and decided about a subject over the past twelve months, in the same neutral words every week. Each is written afresh every Sunday in the same way and with the same checks as any other briefing, except that the question is not read by a model: each topic sets out what to search the minutes for and the twelve months to search. Each week’s briefing is kept at its own address, and the topic’s page shows the newest, with the day it was prepared and the minutes it was written from.

People who aren’t councillors

Minutes also name members of the public (people who speak, ask questions, present petitions, apply for planning permission or object) and council staff. Briefings and pages are limited to councillors’ own words and actions. Other people’s names are replaced with “[name removed]” automatically before the minutes are searched, shown or sent to the AI model, and roles are kept (“[name removed], Chief Executive”). The rules that find them miss some names; a name that is reported is removed by hand. Declarations of interest are given as who declared, the item and the kind of interest, without the reason given, which can describe a councillor’s family or health; the link to the council’s minutes has the rest. The council’s own record is unchanged; it remains the council’s responsibility. If you are named and something should not be shown, ask for it to be removed.
